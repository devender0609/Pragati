## §A — the `number_grid` representation

A supercell is a cell larger than every one of its neighbours. That
definition is about **adjacency**, and adjacency is a property of a
layout — unstatable in a number line, a strip or an area model. Hence
the blocker.

**The spec holds the numbers and the layout. It does not hold which
cells are supercells.** That is derivable from the numbers by the
chapter's own rule, and a spec that stored it would let a caption and a
grid disagree — precisely the failure the fraction-strip schema was
rewritten in v0.61 to eliminate. `supercellsFor()` computes them; the
validator recomputes every assertion an author makes and rejects a
mismatch by name.

**Adjacency is declared, not assumed.** The textbook's first supercell
work is a single row, where neighbours are unambiguously left and right.
A rectangular grid raises a real question — does the cell above count? —
so `neighbourhood` states the rule per visual, and a test proves the
same nine numbers give different answers under different rules. Picking
one silently would have made the mathematics depend on an implementation
detail.

**Strictly greater.** A tie produces no supercell. It is the case an
author is likeliest to get wrong by eye, which is the argument for
computing rather than declaring.

`NumberGridFigure` renders it. The SVG is `aria-hidden` and a visually
hidden `<table>` carries the numbers, because a screen reader handed a
flat list of values learns nothing about adjacency — which is the entire
mathematical content.

## §B — §3.2 Supercells

Authored in the book's order, immediately after §3.1. **§3.3 was
authorable in v0.79** on the existing `NumberLineSpec` and is still not
authored, because §3.2 comes first in the book. A test pins the authored
list to `['3.1', '3.2']` so the chapter cannot grow out of order for the
convenience of whichever representation happens to exist.

Three visuals, each declaring its neighbour rule: a single row, a
deliberate tie row asserting **no** supercells, and a three-by-three grid
under the orthogonal rule. Eight-step explanation, three worked
examples, three guided and five independent items, two reasoning tasks,
full teacher notes.

Four new misconceptions, all about the **definition** rather than about
comparing numbers — a student can compare 4188 and 5353 perfectly well
and still get every supercell wrong. Three carry a diagnostic signal;
`neighbourhood_assumed_not_stated` carries `null`, because a single
answer cannot reveal which rule the student used unless the two rules
disagree on that grid.

## What this does not claim

Both sections are `authored_draft`, `competency_proposed`. Number Play
is still not `official_learn_available` — writing a lesson does not
publish one. The drafts teach the ideas the section titles name; they do
not reproduce the textbook's own activities, because Pragati has the
verified contents structure for Chapter 3 and not a page-level reading
of its exercises. Both sets of teacher notes say so.
