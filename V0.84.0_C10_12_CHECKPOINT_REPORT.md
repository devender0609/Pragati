## D/E. Architecture audit — verdict B

Checkpoint 32 gave the geometry layer `GeometryCircle` and `GeometryArc`, so
it can **draw** a circle, an arc, a chord and an angle mark. What it has no
concept of is a **region**. There is no way in `GeometryFigureSpec` to say
that a marked area is the sector rather than the segment, and a fill colour
carries no mathematics — a lesson could shade one and compute the other with
nothing to object.

`circleRegion.ts` adds that semantics. A `CircleRegion` carries its kind, its
central angle, and — where it is a difference — the composition it IS. The
minor segment declares `['sector', 'triangle']` and its area is computed from
that composition; it is never given an area of its own. This is the
checkpoint-56 principle moved into the plane: `CompositeSolidSpec` derived
exposed surface from the joins, and this derives the segment from the
subtraction.

B is claimed for the new semantic capability, not for the helper functions.

## G. Sector derivation

Arc length and sector area are derived together from the one fraction θ/360.
`proportionsAgree` asserts that arc/circumference and sector/disc are both
exactly that share, tested at eight angles, and arc length is bounded between
0 and the circumference. Angles are degrees throughout; θ/360 means nothing
otherwise and the chapter has no occasion for radians.

## H. The segment, structurally

`segment = sector − triangle` is computed, and the validator rejects three
distinct ways of getting it wrong: a segment that declares no composition, a
segment that declares the wrong one, and a segment whose area equals the
sector's — the fixture the brief asked for. The backwards subtraction
`triangle − sector` is tested to come out negative at every allowed angle,
which is what makes that error self-announcing.

The major segment is derived as disc minus minor segment, so the two rebuild
πr² by construction rather than by coincidence.

## I. Triangle areas

Each is derived from a construction the source supports, never from a
trigonometric area formula:

- **60°** — the isosceles triangle with a 60° apex has 60° base angles, so it
  is equilateral of side r: (√3/4)r².
- **90°** — the radii are perpendicular, so it is right-angled with legs r:
  ½r².
- **120°** — the perpendicular from the centre splits it into two 30-60-90
  triangles; half the chord is r sin 60° and the height r cos 60°, giving
  (√3/4)r².

The 60° and 120° triangles therefore have the **same area** despite being
different shapes. That is true, it surprises students, and a practice item
uses it to show why each case has to be derived rather than read off the
picture.

Angles outside {60, 90, 120} throw.

## J/L/M. Reconciliation, invariants and recomputation

Figure and formula angles are reconciled explicitly, and a test confirms that
60° and 120° give different regions — which is why the drift matters.

Invariants checked at three radii and all three angles: triangle ≥ 0,
segment ≥ 0, segment ≤ sector ≤ disc, and minor + major = disc.

Areas stay **exact and symbolic** as (rat + pi·π + root3·√3)·r², so the 90°
segment is `1/4π − 1/2` and the 60° segment is `1/6π − 1/4√3` rather than a
rounded decimal. Every authored figure is recomputed: 55.94, 559.81, 3.26,
30.10, 123.84, 10.27, 17.75 for segments, and the sector and arc values
alongside.

Combined figures: the square less a corner quarter is 42.06, and the square
less four corner quarters of half the radius is **also 42.06** — because a
quarter of a radius-14 disc equals a whole radius-7 disc. The equality is
asserted in a test so it reads as the fact it is rather than a copy-paste
slip.

The perimeter is traced, not adjusted: 49.99 against the square's own 56, and
a test confirms the traced value is *shorter*, since the arc replaces two
straight sides.
