# Class 7 to Class 8 — transition audit

Written from the complete Class 7 decomposition and all **14 Class 8 chapters**
of Ganita Prakash Part I and Part II, every page read and every picture-carried
page rendered and looked at. **[S]** source observation, **[P]** Pragati
recommendation.

## Ratio and proportion — the discontinuity

**[S] Ratio as a named mathematical object first appears in Class 8 Part I
chapter 7.** Classes 1-7 hold a great deal of precursor proportional and
relational reasoning — scaling, comparing quantities, unit conversion, similar
figures — but the ratio itself, its simplest form, the rule of three and sharing
in a given ratio all arrive there. Precursor relational reasoning is not a
formal named ratio, and the distinction matters for sequencing.
**[S]** Part II chapter 3 extends it: ratios in map scales, multi-term ratios
written with the proportion notation, pie charts built by turning each share
into an angle, and **inverse proportion** as a second kind of relationship —
"going by bicycle is 3 times faster and the time decreases by the same factor".
**[P]** Sequence Part I chapter 7 early. Percentages (II.1) and Proportional
Reasoning-2 (II.3) both rest on it.

## Powers

**[S]** Class 8 opens with squares, cubes and their roots, then formalises
repeated multiplication as exponent notation.
**[S]** §2.3 **motivates zero and negative exponents by preserving the exponent
pattern** — the value is defined so the division rule already established keeps
working. That is what this section does; no claim is made about how every
earlier rule in the programme was justified, and the decomposed Classes 1-7
evidence already contains deductive geometry, parity impossibility arguments and
algebraic reasoning.
**[S]** Part II chapter 1 meets the same idea from the other side: interest
added back each year is repeated proportional change, and the chapter derives an
expression for the total.

## Number systems

**[S]** Chapter 3 makes **representation itself the object of study** — tally
marks, Egyptian, Roman, Mesopotamian, Mayan and Chinese numerals — then
generalises place value to an arbitrary base. Class 7 uses place value; Class 8
asks what it is.

## Geometry and proof

**[S] No prerequisite gap.** Class 7 proves the triangle angle sum and states the
congruence conditions; Class 8 uses congruence as a working tool, proving every
quadrilateral property rather than measuring it.
**[S]** Part II chapter 2 adds a second proof style: the Baudhayana-Pythagoras
theorem is established by **area dissection** — cut pieces of two squares cover
the square on the hypotenuse — and the same chapter argues that **no terminating
decimal squares to 2**, giving the programme its first length that is not a
fraction.
**[S]** Part II chapter 7 proves its area formulas by dissection too, and uses
the Class 7 parallel-line results to show that triangles on a common base
between parallels have equal area.

## Algebra

**[S] Identities are new.** Class 7 ends at equations solved systematically;
Class 8 extends the distributive property to products of expressions, names an
identity, and derives the square-of-a-sum and difference-of-squares results.
**[S]** Part I chapter 5 proves the divisibility tests algebraically from place
value — Class 6 justified them informally — and Part II chapter 6 then uses that
algebra to explain think-of-a-number tricks, generalise number pyramids and
calendar grids, and settle which arrangement of digits gives the largest product.
**[P]** Author I.6 → I.5 → II.6 as one algebraic-proof sequence.

## Data

**[S]** Class 7 introduces mean and median and their limits. Class 8 **proves how
the mean behaves** — as the balance point where distances either side cancel, and
algebraically for what happens when every value is shifted or doubled — and adds
the line graph for change over time, with statements tested as always, sometimes
or never true. An extension with a new reasoning demand, not a repeat.

## Spatial geometry

**[S]** Part II chapter 4 is not a procedural sequence. Fractals are shapes
defined by iterating a rule; the solids work runs two ways between a solid and
its net, and then uses the net to find a shortest path across a surface;
projections and isometric drawing represent three dimensions on paper.
**[P]** Author it for spatial reasoning, not for formulas.

## Open items for human decision

**[S]** Two numbered sections set no objective a student can be assessed against,
and they raise **different** questions. Part I §1.3 "A Pinch of History" is
history about mathematics. Part II §2.6 "A Long-Standing Open Problem" presents
a question nobody has answered. They carry two distinct policies rather than one.
