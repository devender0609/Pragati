## E/F. Architecture — verdict B

The geometry layer could already draw circles, arcs and chords (checkpoint
32) and had `CircleRegion` for areas (checkpoint 66). What it had no concept
of was a **line's relation to a circle** or a **point's position relative to
one**. Nothing could say a line is tangent rather than secant, and nothing
could derive how many tangents a point admits.

`tangency.ts` adds that, generally: `positionOf` (interior / on / exterior
from |OA|² against r²), `relationOf` (secant / tangent / misses),
`isTangentAt`, `tangentCount`, `tangentSegmentLengthSq`, and validators for a
tangent figure and for a two-tangent figure. All exact integer arithmetic —
squared distances only, no square roots.

B is claimed for the new semantics, which are reusable for any
circle-and-line geometry, not for the helper functions.

## H. Tangent definition

Defined by incidence — exactly one shared point — not by touching. The lesson
argues why: a line passing within a hair of the circle looks identical to one
meeting it once, so appearance cannot decide and counting can. The limiting
secant is given and **labelled motivation in the same breath**; a worked
example ends by stating it establishes nothing.

## I. Theorem 1 — radius ⟂ tangent

Proved from the definition. Every other point Q of the tangent lies outside
the circle (else two shared points and the line is a secant), so OQ > OP for
every such Q, so OP is the shortest distance from O to the line, and the
shortest distance from a point to a line is along the perpendicular.

The shortest-distance step is named as an earlier result used as a tool, not
left magic. A worked example shows why the same argument fails for a secant —
points inside are *nearer* than the radius — which demonstrates the
hypothesis doing real work.

**No right-angle mark appears in the proof lesson.** The lesson says
explicitly that such a mark records the conclusion and would assume it, and a
test asserts that sentence is present.

## J. Point location and tangent count

Derived, never authored: `tangentCountFor` reads the position and returns
0 / 1 / 2. The centre itself returns 0, since it is interior. The interior
case is given a *reason* — every line through an interior point meets the
circle twice and so is a secant — and a test asserts the lesson does not stop
at "it is impossible".

## K. Theorem 2 — equal tangents

Proved by RHS. OA = OB as radii; the right angles at A and B come **from
theorem 1**, applied at each point of contact; OP is common, being literally
the same segment. The correspondence O↔O, A↔B, P↔P is stated, and PA is
identified as corresponding to PB.

A worked example establishes that without theorem 1 the triangles are not
known to be right-angled, RHS is unavailable, and the proof stalls — which is
why the ordering is not arbitrary.

## M. Congruence correspondence

`corresponds` maps the vertex triples and checks a named side pair. PA↔PB and
OA↔OB pass; PA↔OB and OA↔PB fail. A practice item uses the false one.

## N/O. Figure and application fidelity

The canonical figure is a 3-4-5 triangle scaled by five: radius 15, external
point at (25, 0), contact points at (9, ±12). OA = 15, PA = 20, OP = 25, all
whole numbers, with the perpendicularity confirmed by an exact dot product of
zero and the two tangent segments confirmed equal by squared distance.

Negative fixtures break each condition in turn: an interior point claiming
two tangents, a contact point off the circle, coincident contact points, and
a tangent-looking line whose stated contact point is wrong.

Applications recompute (15, 25, 20), (8, 17, 15) and (5, 13, 12), and the
angle results 110°, 120° and 6√2. The tangent-length expression is kept tied
to its right triangle rather than presented as a formula, and the lesson says
why. A test confirms the thin phrase "since it is a tangent" appears only as
the thing being improved.

Tangent line and tangent segment are distinguished explicitly, and the
one-way direction of theorem 2 is stated — PA = PB does not prove tangency.
